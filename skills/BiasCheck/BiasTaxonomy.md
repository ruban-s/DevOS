# Bias category reference

The shelf the Check flow pulls from. Consult per artifact — a typical source triggers three to seven entries across strata, never the whole shelf.

Every entry carries a working definition, the observable tell, and a concrete illustration.

---

## Stratum 1 — flaws inside the evidence

### Producer-funded pull

**What it is:** whoever bankrolled or built the study profits when the conclusion lands.

**Tell:** the sponsor's offering reads as the implied remedy for the documented problem.

**Illustration:** a consultancy selling workforce-transition services surveys executives and concludes executives must urgently buy workforce-transition services.

### Unrepresentative panel

**What it is:** respondents don't stand in for the claimed population.

**Tells:** client-list or opt-in recruitment; undisclosed response rate; open-link self-selection; convenience panels dressed in margin-of-error language; frame swaps between sample ("executives") and headline ("CEOs").

**Illustration:** "1,000 US executives" resolves to a mixed roster of C-suite, HR, and investor titles drawn from the vendor's marketing reach.

### Loaded instrument

**What it is:** wording, scale, or ordering steers answers.

**Tells:** near-unanimous agreement on a substantive item (over ~95% nearly always means a trivial yes/no); charged vocabulary in the stem; lopsided forced choices; item text withheld while toplines circulate.

**Illustration:** "Do you expect AI to touch headcount in any way within 24 months?" returns 99% yes; coverage reports "99% planning AI layoffs."

### Respondent performance

**What it is:** participants supply the respectable answer the setting cues, whatever they privately believe.

**Tells:** sponsor identity visible during fielding; topics with strong professional norms (denying AI plans reads as out-of-touch); no stated anonymity.

**Illustration:** executives under board pressure on AI posture affirm workforce-change plans because denial signals cluelessness.

### Stated intent versus behavior

**What it is:** forecasts of one's own future actions predict weakly, especially executive forecasts of organizational moves.

**Tells:** forward verbs ("expect to", "plan to"); no calibration against the same panel's prior forecasts; multi-year horizons.

**Illustration:** three-quarters of executives in 2023 foresaw production agents by end of 2024; most missed. Later waves repeat the shape.

### Correlation dressed as cause

**What it is:** co-occurrence presented with causal verbs.

**Tells:** two unrelated cross-sections compared as a panel; verbs like "driving" or "causing" over snapshots; visible confounds unaddressed.

**Illustration:** worker thriving falls from 66% (2024) to 44% (2026) across different samples and macro regimes; coverage blames AI alone.

### Baseless superlative

**What it is:** a striking figure with no baseline to judge it against.

**Tells:** large percentages without prior-year or pre-event comparators; "unprecedented" with no series shown; AI-attribution claims lacking pre-AI rates.

**Illustration:** "99% foresee layoffs within two years" — against a background corporate-churn rate already in the nineties, the figure may measure baseline turnover wearing an AI label.

### Gated methods

**What it is:** only toplines circulate; instrument, dataset, or protocol stays private.

**Tells:** report locked behind a lead form; a paragraph of marketing copy where methods belong; no item wordings or distributions; a press release doing all downstream work.

**Illustration:** a glossy sixty-page PDF charts toplines but prints no question text; trade press cites the charts; mainstream cites trade press; nobody can audit the items.

### Survivor-only sampling

**What it is:** failures and exits leave the frame, inflating the studied outcome.

**Tells:** "successful adopters of X" used to prescribe X; holders who didn't sell; active customers minus churn.

**Illustration:** "top startups all use AI tooling" — against near-universal tooling adoption, sampling on the outcome explains the pattern.

### Snapshot-mismatched trend

**What it is:** year-over-year deltas computed across different respondent pools as if they were one panel.

**Tells:** "X% said Y in 2024, Z% in 2026" with no panel continuity; undisclosed wording drift; shifted macro backdrop between waves.

**Illustration:** two HR-sentiment waves two years apart, different panels, the gap attributed to the studied variable while population shift could carry most of it.

## Stratum 2 — producer interests

### Commercial tie

**What it is:** the producer's revenue leans on the report's conclusion.

**Tell:** recommendations read as a service brochure.

**Illustration:** a consultancy's report prescribes consulting; the consultancy sells consulting.

### Mission tie

**What it is:** a stated ideological or political mission aligns with the finding.

**Tell:** an advocacy outfit's research lands exactly on its public position.

**Illustration:** an industry-backed group studying regulation finds regulation harmful.

### Perennial verdict

**What it is:** successive reports converge on one recommendation regardless of shifting methods.

**Tell:** methodology rotates yearly; the buying advice never moves.

**Illustration:** five annual "state of X" editions each declare this year the inflection point for the vendor's category.

### Missing disclosure

**What it is:** the artifact never states its own relationship to the conclusion.

**Tell:** no research-background section, no funder note, no customer roster — the reader must investigate separately.

**Illustration:** a survey report with no methods appendix on funding, sampling vendor, or client ties.

### House experts

**What it is:** quoted authorities sit on the producer's payroll, board, or retainer.

**Tell:** every independent voice traces to the same entity.

**Illustration:** a safety report's three "leading researchers" all draw checks from the publishing think tank.

## Stratum 3 — retelling distortions

### Headline-versus-source drift

**What it is:** each hop strengthens the claim — source to body to headline.

**Tell:** noun swaps ("executives" → "CEOs"), verb swaps ("expect" → "plan"), scope swaps ("affect" → "replace").

**Illustration:** source "executives expect AI to touch workforce decisions" → body "executives plan AI layoffs" → headline "CEOs to fire workers over AI."

### Hotter reframing

**What it is:** identical numbers, editorially warmer diction matching outlet stance.

**Tell:** systematic word choice pushing one direction ("surveillance" for "behavioral analytics").

**Illustration:** "HR teams plan behavioral-analytics adoption" retold as "HR turns to surveillance."

### Hop-by-hop amplification

**What it is:** vendor release → trade outlet → mainstream → social, methods shedding at every transfer.

**Tell:** citations point at intermediaries, never the primary.

**Illustration:** a "99% foresee some workforce change" topline arrives at social feeds as "all CEOs planning mass layoffs."

### Causal verbs on correlational numbers

**What it is:** "driven by", "due to", "caused by" attached to snapshot or survey data.

**Tell:** causal connective with no design supporting it.

**Illustration:** wellbeing fell while adoption rose; coverage declares adoption caused the fall.

### Vanished confounds

**What it is:** plausible rival explanations go unmentioned.

**Tell:** three obvious confounds the piece never names.

**Illustration:** a wellbeing drop blamed on AI with no mention of inflation, return-to-office mandates, election cycles, or post-COVID burnout moving in the same window.

### Topline-only citation

**What it is:** the retelling quotes the number while dropping the source's own caveats.

**Tell:** a limitations section exists upstream and goes uncited.

**Illustration:** "non-probability sample, do not generalize" appears in the report and nowhere in the article that generalizes it.

### Loaded coloring

**What it is:** adjectives smuggle a verdict the numbers don't state.

**Tell:** "ominous", "stark", "dire" doing the argumentative work.

**Illustration:** "44% report dissatisfaction" framed as "a stark warning" — without a benchmark, 44% is neither high nor low.

### Single-frame telling

**What it is:** one interpretation presented where the numbers admit several.

**Tell:** no dissenting voice, no alternative frame explored.

**Illustration:** identical survey figures could read as "leaders finally take AI seriously" or "bosses plot replacements" — the piece prints exactly one.

---

## Reading protocol

Walk the shelf top to bottom per artifact. Expect a handful of hits, not a sweep. Never force a miss into a hit; never skip a genuine hit.

**Common signatures:**

- Funded study with viral pickup → producer pull plus loaded instrument plus headline drift plus causal verbs
- Scholarly paper with niche pickup → panel limits plus missing baseline plus topline-only citation
- Release reprinted verbatim → most of stratum 2 plus hop amplification
- Opinion wearing analysis clothes → wrong instrument entirely; route elsewhere
